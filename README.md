 Tech Store Sales Analysis

**Exploring sales performance, customer satisfaction, product returns, and advertising efficiency using Python.**

  About the Project

This project started as a way for me to put my Python data analysis skills into practice.

I've been learning Python, NumPy, Pandas, Matplotlib, and Seaborn, and I wanted to go beyond following tutorials or memorizing functions. I wanted to work with a dataset, ask meaningful questions, and see what I could discover from the data.

That's how this Tech Store Sales Analysis project came together.

I explored a tech store's sales data to understand which products generate the most revenue, which categories and regions perform well, how customer ratings vary, which products have higher return rates, and how advertising spending compares with revenue.

The goal wasn't just to create charts. It was to understand what the numbers were saying and use them to suggest possible business improvements.

   Dataset Overview

The project uses a practice dataset named `tech_store_sales_practice.csv`.

It contains 150 sales records and 10 columns covering sales dates, products, regions, revenue, advertising expenditure, customer ratings, and product returns.

Some of the important columns include:

- `Date` — the date of the sales record.
- `Category` — the product category.
- `Product` — the product being analyzed.
- `Region` — the sales region.
- `Units_Sold` — the number of units sold.
- `Unit_Price_USD` — the unit price in US dollars.
- `Revenue_USD` — the recorded revenue in US dollars.
- `Ad_Spend_USD` — advertising expenditure.
- `Customer_Rating` — the recorded customer rating.
- `Returned_Units` — the number of returned units.

This is a practice dataset, so the findings demonstrate my analysis process rather than establish facts about a real retailer.

 What I Did

1. Data inspection and cleaning

Before analyzing anything, I needed to understand the dataset.

I used Pandas to inspect the first few rows, examine the data types, review summary statistics, check for missing values and duplicate records, and convert the date column into a proper datetime format.

I also checked important data-quality conditions, including whether recorded revenue matched units sold multiplied by unit price.

This stage taught me that analysis should begin with understanding the data, not immediately creating charts.

2. Exploratory data analysis

After inspecting the dataset, I started asking questions using Pandas.

I grouped the data by product, category, and region to calculate total revenue, units sold, advertising expenditure, returned units, and average customer ratings.

I also identified the five highest-revenue products and calculated overall sales metrics.

This helped me become more comfortable with `groupby()`, `agg()`, `sum()`, `mean()`, `sort_values()`, and `head()`.

3. Data visualization

I used Matplotlib and Seaborn to make the results easier to understand.

The visualizations included:

- **Top-five product revenue bar chart:** Compared the revenue generated by the leading products.
- **Category revenue chart:** Showed how revenue differed across product categories.
- **Regional revenue chart:** Compared revenue across sales regions.
- **Daily revenue line chart:** Helped me observe fluctuations in daily revenue.
- **Monthly revenue line chart:** Made it easier to compare revenue across months.
- **Correlation heatmap:** Helped me explore linear relationships between numerical variables.
- **Advertising spend vs. revenue scatter plot:** Helped me assess whether higher advertising expenditure appeared to be associated with higher revenue.
- **Average customer rating by category:** Compared recorded customer ratings across product categories.

Creating these charts helped me understand that choosing the right visualization matters. A bar chart is useful for comparing categories, while a line chart is better suited to showing changes over time.

4. Product return analysis

I wanted to investigate returns more carefully, rather than simply compare the number of returned units.

I grouped the data by product and calculated total units sold and returned. Then I created a return-rate metric:

`Return Rate (%) = (Returned Units / Units Sold) × 100`

This allowed me to compare products fairly, even when they had different sales volumes.

One of my findings was that the **Bluetooth Speaker had the highest return rate at approximately 4.55%**, with four returned units out of 88 sold.

The Wireless Mouse followed at approximately 3.85%, with four returned units out of 104 sold.

These results suggested that the Bluetooth Speaker and Wireless Mouse deserved further investigation. However, the data alone could not tell me why customers returned them.

5. Revenue and returns together

Next, I combined revenue, units sold, returned units, and return rate into one product-level summary.

This allowed me to examine sales performance and returns together instead of treating them as unrelated metrics.

For example, the Laptop Pro generated the highest total revenue in the dataset, at $79,200, with one returned unit out of 66 sold.

The Smartwatch Series X generated $45,300 in revenue from 151 units sold and had no recorded returns.

These findings helped me think about how a business might balance revenue performance, sales volume, and product returns when deciding which products to investigate or promote.

6. Advertising efficiency

I also investigated how much revenue each product generated relative to its advertising expenditure.

I calculated the revenue-to-ad-spend ratio, also known as **Return on Ad Spend (ROAS)**:

`ROAS = Total Revenue / Total Advertising Spend`

For example, a ratio of 5 means that $1 in advertising expenditure corresponds to $5 in recorded revenue.

In my initial comparison of five products, the Mechanical Keyboard had a ratio of approximately 5.33, while the Wireless Mouse had a ratio of approximately 1.24.

This gave me a way to compare advertising efficiency across products.

However, revenue-to-ad-spend ratio is not the same as profit. It doesn't account for product costs, shipping, salaries, or other expenses. It also doesn't prove that advertising caused the revenue.

Business Recommendations

Based on the patterns I observed, I came up with several recommendations.

**1. Investigate products with high return rates.**

The Bluetooth Speaker and Wireless Mouse deserve further investigation. I would review customer complaints, product quality, product descriptions, and supplier records before deciding whether to change suppliers or discontinue a product.

**2. Investigate growth opportunities carefully.**

The Mechanical Keyboard showed a relatively high revenue-to-ad-spend ratio in my initial comparison. Before increasing its advertising budget, I would investigate customer feedback, demand, product quality, and profit margins.

**3. Review advertising efficiency.**

The Wireless Mouse generated relatively little revenue compared with its advertising expenditure in the results I examined. I would investigate its advertising campaigns, conversion rates, audience targeting, and overall costs before deciding whether to change the budget.

**4. Consider revenue and customer satisfaction together.**

A product generating high revenue isn't automatically the most profitable or the most satisfying for customers. I would combine sales performance, returns, ratings, and costs before making major inventory or marketing decisions.

These are recommendations for further investigation, not definitive conclusions about product quality or profitability.

Limitations and Future Improvements

Although this project helped me learn a lot, there are several things I would improve with more data and experience.

- Add product costs and other operating expenses to estimate profit margins.
- Include customer feedback or return reasons to understand why products are returned.
- Analyze advertising impressions, clicks, conversions, and campaign details.

- Build an interactive dashboard to make the findings easier to explore.
- Explore forecasting or machine learning after developing a stronger understanding of the data.
